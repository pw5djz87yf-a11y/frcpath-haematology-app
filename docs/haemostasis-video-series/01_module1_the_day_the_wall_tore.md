# Module 1: The Day the Wall Tore
### Haemostasis overview: vascular, platelet, coagulation and fibrinolytic phases

**Series:** *The Chronicles of Vessel-on-the-Hill* · **Runtime:** about 9 min · **Draft v0.2 (decisions from your review applied)**

**Learning outcomes.** By the end of this module the viewer can:
1. Define haemostasis as a balance: stopping bleeding locally while keeping blood fluid everywhere else.
2. Name the four phases in order, say what each achieves, and explain that they overlap.
3. Explain why intact endothelium is antithrombotic, and what injury exposes.
4. Outline the cell-based model (initiation, amplification, propagation) and how it relates to the lab "roads".
5. State what PT, APTT and TT test, and apply this to haemophilia A.

**Colour key (fixed):** 🔴 red = clotting · 🔵 blue = fibrinolysis · 🟡 gold = inhibitors/gatekeepers (gold with a blue outline = inhibitors of fibrinolysis) · ⚪ grey = lab tests

## Part B: Narration script

*Length about 1,470 spoken words (hook lines included). At about 165 words per minute that gives a runtime of about 9 minutes, as agreed. **[Story]** = story voice; **[Science]** = clinical voice. **Hook lines are in bold** and are repeated at the end of each scene.*

### Scene 0: Cold open (0:00–0:30) [Story]

High on its hill, the kingdom of Vessel-on-the-Hill wraps a great stone wall around a red river. On peaceful days the wall's lanterns glow softly, and the message to everyone is simple: keep flowing.

Then, *crack*. A siege stone splits the wall, and the river begins to spill.

What happens next always happens in the same order. The walls squeeze. The Guards rush in. A torch relay races along the castle road to wake the Mayor, who spins a net. And when the wall has healed, a demolition crew clears the net away.

This is the story of the day the wall tore.

### Scene 1: Title and the four phases (0:30–1:00) [Science]

This is **haemostasis**: the body's way of stopping bleeding at a site of injury while keeping blood fluid everywhere else. Remember both halves of that sentence. Haemostasis is not just clotting. It is clotting in the right place, for the right length of time.

We describe four phases: **vascular, platelet, coagulation and fibrinolytic.** The vascular and platelet phases together are called *primary haemostasis*. Coagulation is *secondary haemostasis*.

> **Hook: "Stop the bleed, keep the flow."**

### Scene 2: The peaceful wall (1:00–1:50)

**[Story]** Before any tear, the wall itself is the kingdom's chief peacekeeper.

**[Science]** Intact endothelium is actively *antithrombotic*. It releases **prostacyclin** and **nitric oxide**, which dilate the vessel and keep platelets quiet. Its surface carries **heparan sulphate**, which strongly accelerates antithrombin, and **thrombomodulin**, which captures thrombin and redirects it to activate protein C. It expresses **tissue factor pathway inhibitor**, it breaks down the platelet agonist ADP using the surface enzyme CD39, and it secretes **tissue plasminogen activator**.

The molecules that start clotting, **collagen** and **tissue factor**, sit *behind* the endothelium, out of contact with flowing blood.

> **Hook: "A healthy wall keeps the peace."**

### Scene 3: The vascular phase (1:50–2:40)

**[Story]** The stone strikes. The wall flinches and squeezes, narrowing the gap. Behind the broken stones, an alarm bell is suddenly exposed to the river.

**[Science]** The vascular phase is immediate. A local reflex causes **vasoconstriction**, which slows blood flow. It is reinforced by **thromboxane A₂** and **serotonin** released from platelets, and by **endothelin** from the endothelium. Vasoconstriction matters most in small vessels.

Injury also exposes what was hidden: **subendothelial collagen**, **von Willebrand factor** bound to the matrix, and **tissue factor** on cells such as fibroblasts and smooth muscle cells. Tissue factor is the trigger for coagulation.

Note that these events start *at the same moment*. The four phases overlap, and we separate them only to make them easier to understand.

> **Hooks: "First, the walls squeeze." "Tissue factor is the alarm bell behind the wall."**

### Scene 4: The platelet phase (2:40–3:50)

**[Story]** The Platelet Guards arrive. They throw ropes to the wall and tie on. Then they raise spiked shields and link arms with one another. The gap is plugged, but a line of guards can still be pushed aside.

**[Science]** Primary haemostasis has three steps.

**Adhesion.** Under the high shear of arterial and small-vessel flow, platelet **glycoprotein Ib**, part of the GPIb-IX-V complex, binds **von Willebrand factor** attached to exposed collagen. Platelets also bind collagen directly through **GPVI** and integrin **α2β1**.

**Activation.** Platelets change shape, release granule contents such as **ADP**, and make **thromboxane A₂**. These recruit more platelets. The activated platelet also flips negatively charged **phosphatidylserine** to its outer surface. This surface is the stage on which coagulation will be built.

**Aggregation.** Activated **glycoprotein IIb/IIIa** binds **fibrinogen** and von Willebrand factor, which bridge one platelet to the next.

The Rope has one more job: in plasma, von Willebrand factor **carries factor VIII** and protects it from rapid clearance.

The platelet plug forms within minutes, but it is fragile. It needs a fibrin net to hold it in place.

> **Hooks: "Rope ties guards to the wall." "GPIb grabs the rope; IIb/IIIa links guard to guard." "Guards plug the gap: quick but fragile."**

### Scene 5: The coagulation phase (3:50–5:30)

**[Story]** At the alarm bell, the first messenger lights a torch. Each messenger lights the next, and the flame runs along the castle road. But a messenger can only stand on the guards' shields if it is painted with magic ink. The messengers are built in the Liver Workshop, but four of them, II, VII, IX and X, cannot grip the ink until the Vitamin K Forge has put the final edge on them. At the end of the road waits Thrombin the Mayor.

**[Science]** Coagulation turns the fragile platelet plug into a stable clot. Most clotting factors circulate as inactive precursors of enzymes. Each activated factor activates the next, so a small signal is amplified into a large burst.

In the body, coagulation starts when **tissue factor binds factor VIIa**. This complex activates factors **X** and **IX**, and a *small* amount of thrombin forms. This is **initiation**.

That small amount of thrombin then **amplifies** the process. It fully activates platelets, and it activates factors **V**, **VIII** and **XI**, releasing VIII from its von Willebrand factor carrier.

In **propagation**, two complexes assemble on the activated platelet surface. The **tenase** complex, IXa with VIIIa, activates factor X. The **prothrombinase** complex, Xa with Va, converts prothrombin to thrombin. The result is a *burst* of thrombin.

These complexes need **calcium**, which bridges the factors to the negatively charged phospholipid. That is why blood taken into a citrate tube does not clot: citrate binds calcium, and the laboratory adds calcium back to start the test.

Factors **II, VII, IX and X**, together with the natural anticoagulants **protein C and protein S**, are made in the liver, but they only become functional once **vitamin K** has completed them. Vitamin K is the cofactor for γ-carboxylation, which gives them the calcium-binding Gla domains. Warfarin blocks the recycling of vitamin K, so it takes days to work fully: the factors already in the circulation have to be cleared first.

Finally, thrombin converts soluble **fibrinogen** into **fibrin** and activates **factor XIII**, which cross-links the fibrin into a stable mesh.

> **Hooks: "Each torch lights the next, and the flame grows." "No ink, no stick." "Ten, nine, seven, two: finished by K." "The Mayor turns thread into net."**

### Scene 6: The Gatekeepers (5:30–6:15)

**[Story]** A relay that never stopped would set the whole kingdom ablaze. So at every gate stands a Gatekeeper, dressed in gold.

**[Science]** Natural anticoagulants keep the clot local. **Antithrombin** inactivates thrombin and factor Xa, and to a lesser extent IXa, XIa and XIIa. Heparin, and heparan sulphate on the vessel wall, accelerate it enormously.

**Protein C** is activated when thrombin binds **thrombomodulin** on healthy endothelium. So once the Mayor steps outside the breach, he changes sides. Activated protein C, with its cofactor **protein S**, cleaves and inactivates factors **Va and VIIIa**. **Tissue factor pathway inhibitor** switches off the starting complex early on.

When a Gatekeeper is missing, the result is *thrombosis*, not bleeding.

> **Hooks: "Antithrombin locks the Mayor's door; heparin speeds the key." "C cuts, S supports: Five and Eight stop."**

### Scene 7: The fibrinolytic phase (6:15–7:00)

**[Story]** When the wall is mended, Foreman tPA blows his whistle. The Demolition Crew, who have been camped beside the net all along, take it apart and leave a little rubble behind.

**[Science]** Fibrinolysis removes the clot as the vessel heals. **Tissue plasminogen activator**, released from endothelium, converts **plasminogen** into **plasmin**. It works far better when both are bound to fibrin, which keeps lysis local. Plasmin breaks fibrin into fibrin degradation products. Cross-linked fibrin, made by factor XIII, breaks down into fragments containing two linked D-domains: **D-dimer**. A raised D-dimer tells us that fibrin was formed and then broken down.

The crew has brakes too. **PAI-1** inhibits tPA, and **α2-antiplasmin** inhibits plasmin.

Formation and breakdown of fibrin overlap in time. The crew's tools are on site from the start.

> **Hooks: "Build the net, then clear the net." "D-dimer is rubble from a stitched net."**

### Scene 8: The Inspectors and a worked example (7:00–7:40)

**[Story]** When something goes wrong, the kingdom sends its Inspectors, dressed in grey.

**[Science]** In the laboratory we split coagulation into two roads that join. The **prothrombin time**, PT, is started with tissue factor. It tests the short, *extrinsic* road through **factor VII**, then the common road: X, V, II and fibrinogen. The **INR** is derived from the PT to standardise warfarin monitoring.

The **activated partial thromboplastin time**, APTT, tests the long, *intrinsic* road, **XII, XI, IX and VIII**, and then the same common road. The **thrombin time** tests only the final step, from fibrinogen to fibrin.

These roads are a laboratory model. In the body, factor XII is not needed: its deficiency prolongs the APTT but does not cause bleeding.

**Worked example:** in **haemophilia A**, messenger VIII is missing from the long road. The **APTT is prolonged; the PT is normal.**

> **Hooks: "PT: short road, Seven." "APTT: long road: Twelve, Eleven, Nine, Eight." "TT watches the Mayor's last job." "Eight missing on the long road: APTT long, PT normal."**

### Scene 9: Recap (7:40–8:15) [Story]

Let's walk the wall one more time.

*Stop the bleed, keep the flow.* A healthy wall keeps the peace. First, the walls squeeze, and the alarm bell rings. Rope ties guards to the wall. Guards plug the gap: quick but fragile. Each torch lights the next. No ink, no stick. Ten, nine, seven, two: finished by K. The Mayor turns thread into net. C cuts, S supports. Build the net, then clear the net.

And in haemophilia A: Eight missing on the long road, APTT long, PT normal.

Next time: the Platelet Guards, up close.

## Part E: Single-best-answer questions

### Q1
On a peaceful day in Vessel-on-the-Hill, Thrombin the Mayor wanders out onto an undamaged stretch of wall. There, he is captured by a wall protein that makes him switch sides and wake a gold Gatekeeper. Which endothelial molecule is responsible?

A. Tissue factor
B. Thrombomodulin
C. von Willebrand factor
D. Plasminogen activator inhibitor-1
E. Heparan sulphate

**Answer: B. Thrombomodulin.**
*Explanation:* Thrombomodulin on intact endothelium binds thrombin. This changes thrombin's specificity: it stops cleaving fibrinogen and activating platelets, and instead activates protein C (a process enhanced by the endothelial protein C receptor). Activated protein C, with protein S, inactivates Va and VIIIa. **A** Tissue factor is procoagulant and normally hidden in the subendothelium. **C** vWF promotes platelet adhesion and carries factor VIII. **D** PAI-1 inhibits tPA and uPA (an antifibrinolytic). **E** Heparan sulphate is anticoagulant, but it acts by accelerating *antithrombin*, not by activating protein C.

### Q2
A swarm of Platelet Guards is swept along the fast-flowing river in a small artery. The first thing that stops them being washed past the breach is a rope tied to the exposed wall. Which interaction mediates this initial tethering under high shear?

A. GPIIb/IIIa binding fibrinogen
B. GPIb-IX-V binding von Willebrand factor
C. GPVI binding collagen
D. P2Y12 binding ADP
E. PAR-1 cleavage by thrombin

**Answer: B. GPIb-IX-V binding von Willebrand factor.**
*Explanation:* At high shear rates, initial platelet tethering depends on GPIbα binding the A1 domain of vWF anchored to subendothelial collagen. This is why VWD and Bernard–Soulier syndrome (GPIb deficiency) cause mucocutaneous bleeding. **C** GPVI (with α2β1) binds collagen directly and is important for *firm* adhesion and activation signalling, but it is not the main tethering mechanism under high shear. **A** GPIIb/IIIa mediates aggregation (deficient in Glanzmann thrombasthenia). **D** P2Y12 is an ADP receptor that amplifies activation (the target of clopidogrel). **E** PAR-1 is a thrombin receptor involved in activation.

### Q3
In the Inspectors' laboratory, a sample of the river is collected into a light-blue-top tube containing 3.2% sodium citrate, and it does not clot. When the Inspector runs the PT, a reagent containing "magic ink" must be added. Which statement best explains this?

A. Citrate inhibits thrombin directly, and calcium displaces it
B. Citrate binds vitamin K, so factors cannot be carboxylated during storage
C. Citrate chelates calcium, which is needed to link vitamin K-dependent factors to negatively charged phospholipid
D. Citrate activates antithrombin, and calcium reverses this
E. Citrate degrades fibrinogen, so calcium is needed to restore it

**Answer: C.**
*Explanation:* Calcium ions bridge the γ-carboxyglutamic acid (Gla) domains of factors II, VII, IX and X to phosphatidylserine on activated platelet (or reagent phospholipid) surfaces. This is needed for the tenase and prothrombinase complexes to assemble. Citrate chelates calcium reversibly, and the test is started by recalcification. The standard ratio is 9 parts blood to 1 part citrate, so under-filled tubes give falsely prolonged results. **A, D** Citrate has no direct effect on thrombin or antithrombin. **B** γ-carboxylation happens in the liver before secretion. Factors in a sample are already carboxylated, and citrate does not bind vitamin K. **E** Citrate does not degrade fibrinogen.

### Q4
After the wall is repaired, the Demolition Crew leave rubble behind. A raised level of this rubble in the blood (D-dimer) indicates which of the following?

A. Thrombin has cleaved fibrinogen, but no fibrin has been cross-linked
B. Plasmin has digested fibrinogen in the circulation, without fibrin being formed
C. Plasmin has degraded fibrin that had been cross-linked by factor XIIIa
D. A pulmonary embolism is present
E. PAI-1 activity is increased

**Answer: C.**
*Explanation:* D-dimer is a fragment containing two D-domains joined by a covalent bond formed by factor XIIIa. It is released only when plasmin degrades *cross-linked fibrin*, so it shows that both thrombin generation and fibrinolysis have occurred. **A** Without cross-linking, D-dimer is not formed. **B** Plasmin digestion of fibrinogen produces fibrinogen degradation products, but not D-dimer. **D** D-dimer is sensitive but *not specific* for venous thromboembolism. It is raised in DIC, surgery, infection, pregnancy, malignancy and older age, so its main value is a negative result in a patient with low clinical probability. **E** Increased PAI-1 would *reduce* fibrinolysis.

### Q5
An 8-year-old boy is brought to clinic with a swollen, painful knee after a minor fall. His maternal uncle had a similar problem. The Inspectors report: platelet count 260 ×10⁹/L, PT 12 s (normal), APTT 58 s (prolonged; reference range about 26–36 s [VERIFY local range]), and Clauss fibrinogen 3.1 g/L. Which messenger is most likely to be missing?

A. Factor II
B. Factor VII
C. Factor VIII
D. Factor XII
E. Factor XIII

**Answer: C. Factor VIII (haemophilia A).**
*Explanation:* An isolated prolonged APTT with a normal PT points to the intrinsic ("long road") factors VIII, IX, XI or XII. Haemarthrosis and a maternal uncle affected (X-linked inheritance) strongly suggest haemophilia, and haemophilia A (factor VIII deficiency) is the most common form. Haemophilia B (factor IX deficiency) gives the same screening pattern and is distinguished by factor assays. VWD should also be excluded, because low vWF can lower factor VIII. **A** Factor II deficiency prolongs both PT and APTT. **B** Factor VII deficiency prolongs the PT only. **D** Factor XII deficiency prolongs the APTT but does not cause bleeding. **E** Factor XIII deficiency gives a normal PT and APTT.
